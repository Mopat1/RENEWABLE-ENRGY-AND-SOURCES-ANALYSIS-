01 · Introduction & research question

How has the deployment of offshore wind energy advanced in recent years, and what are the barriers to further development?

What I assumed · 1

Offshore wind is just onshore wind placed in the sea.

What I assumed · 2

With a 7,500 km coastline, India must already be doing it.

BOTH WRONG
Before I started, I honestly assumed offshore wind was just onshore wind placed in the sea, and that India, with its long coastline, would already be doing it. Both of those assumptions turned out to be wrong.

Finding one: offshore wind is growing fast worldwide. In 2025 the world added about 9.3 gigawatts of offshore wind, bringing the total to around 92 gigawatts, close to the 100-gigawatt milestone. China alone added 6.6 gigawatts, and it holds roughly half of all offshore capacity in the world. And annual installations are forecast to reach around 34 gigawatts a year by 2030.
02 · Key findings · Technology

Not just turbines in water: a different technology

Stronger, steadier winds at sea, so more power per turbine than onshore

Much larger turbines, since there are no roads or land limits on size

Floating foundations now unlock deep water where fixed ones can't reach

Finding two: the technology has improved a lot. Winds at sea are stronger and steadier, so offshore turbines produce more power. Turbines are much larger, because there are no road or land limits on their size. And floating turbines now allow wind farms in deep water, where fixed foundations simply aren't possible.

Finding three, the one that surprised me most: India has zero commissioned offshore wind capacity, despite an estimated 70 gigawatts of potential off Gujarat and Tamil Nadu. Measurements show offshore sites in Tamil Nadu could run at about 50 percent capacity, compared to around 37 percent in Gujarat. And India has a target of 30 gigawatts by 2030.

So why hasn't it happened? The biggest barrier is cost. Offshore wind power in India is estimated at around 9.6 rupees per unit in Tamil Nadu and 10.5 rupees in Gujarat, while current grid tariffs are around 7 rupees. Developers simply can't make the numbers work.
03 · Challenges & impacts · Other barriers

Cost is only part of the story

Tender failure

In Aug 2025, SECI cancelled 4.5 GW of offshore tenders because almost no developers bid.

Supply chain

Few installation vessels, limited port facilities, and heavy dependence on imports.

Global slowdown

Auctions fell to 11.4 GW in 2025; ~25 GW of approved projects outside China are stalled.

Social & environmental

Fishing communities fear losing fishing grounds; construction can disturb marine life.

That showed up clearly in August 2025, when SECI cancelled offshore wind tenders totalling 4.5 gigawatts because almost no developers bid. India also lacks the special installation vessels, port facilities and local supply chain, so most equipment would have to be imported. Globally there's a similar slowdown: auction volumes dropped sharply in 2025, and around 25 gigawatts of approved projects outside China are stuck waiting for investment decisions. There's also a social impact. Fishing communities worry about losing their fishing grounds, and seabed construction can disturb marine life.
04 · Personal reflection: What did I learn?

How my thinking changed

Before

can we build turbines in the sea?

→
After

An economic & policy problem:
cost, infrastructure, investor confidence.

Good natural resources aren't enough. Without ports, vessels, grid connections and finance, the wind stays unused.

What did I learn? I started thinking the challenge was technical, whether we can build turbines in the sea. I now understand the real challenge is economic and institutional. The technology already works; China proves that. The problem is cost, infrastructure and investor confidence. I also realised that having good natural resources isn't enough. India has the wind, but without ports, vessels, grid connections and financial support, that wind stays unused. This changed how I see renewable energy. It's not only an engineering problem, it's a planning and policy problem.
05 · Solutions & recommendations

Three things India should do

1

Back the first projects

Continue financial support. ₹7,500 crore of viability gap funding for the first 1 GW is a strong start.

2

Build the ecosystem

Invest in ports, vessels and local manufacturing, with partners like the India–UK Offshore Wind Taskforce (2026).

3

Communities first

Consult fishing communities early, with fair compensation and shared benefits.

Based on this, I'd recommend three things. First, continue financial support. The government's viability gap funding for the first 1 gigawatt is a good start. Second, invest in ports, installation vessels and local manufacturing to bring costs down, with help from partnerships like the India–UK Offshore Wind Taskforce launched in 2026. Third, involve fishing communities early, through consultation, compensation and shared benefits, so projects are accepted rather than resisted.
06 · Final takeaway

The technology works.
India's real challenge is making it affordable.

Get the first projects right, especially off Tamil Nadu, and the wind at sea could become one of India's strongest clean-energy sources.

Offshore wind has proven itself globally. India's challenge isn't whether it can work, but whether we can make it affordable. If we get the first projects right, especially off Tamil Nadu, the wind out at sea could become one of India's strongest clean energy sources.
